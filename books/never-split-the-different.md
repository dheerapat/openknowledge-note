# Never split the difference

## Be the Mirror

Enter a negotiation with an empty head. Don't expect anything; listen to the other party as much as possible to understand what they want and make them feel heard.

Slow everything down. Rushing makes you look dishonest, as if you haven't fully listened.

Use the late-night DJ voice: calm. Use the energetic voice, like a cool guy smiling while talking. The last voice, rarely used, is the assertive voice. It can be good or bad depending on the situation, so use it wisely.

Mirroring: repeat the last one to three important keywords the other person said. This makes them feel like we're the same and keeps them talking; they'll continue to give you more intel.

### Example

> A: I will have to raise the rent to $200 more starting at the end of this month.\
> B: I'm sorry, $200?\
> A: Yes, to cover the cost of roofing and increasing city taxes.\
> B: Roofing and tax increases?

Start with “I'm sorry” and mirror their last important keyword. Pause 3-4 sec to let the mirror do the work.

## Label the feeling

Labeling the other person's feelings is a form of empathy. You don't have to agree with them, but you can make them feel acknowledged by naming their emotion out loud: "It seems like you had a hard time getting HR to match my number from our last discussion."

More label examples:

- "It sounds like you feel this deadline is unfair."
- "You seem frustrated that things are moving so slowly."
- "It seems like you're worried this deal won't hold up."
- "It sounds like you're proud of what your team pulled off here."

Labels don't have to be negative. Labeling a positive feeling also deepens rapport.

The label clears the roadblock and helps them reveal their true needs, so you can dig deeper and keep the negotiation going.

Do the accusation audit: list the worst things they might think about you up front. It goes straight at their fear and eases it, like saying "we know what you fear, we're here to ease that fear."

More accusation audit examples:

- "You might think I'm a stubborn employee who keeps insisting on the promotion."
- "You might think we're a greedy company that oppresses small ones."
- "You probably think I'm just another salesperson trying to squeeze you for a commission."
- "I know you probably think I don't care about the timeline, only the money."

Don't use all the techniques bluntly. Practice until you can fold them into your usual negotiation. It's like learning each instrument individually, but in the end you have to perform in a band with harmony.

## Beware Yes, Master No

Being forced to say yes makes people uncomfortable. Yes-oriented questions like "Do you like drinking water?", "Do you like to have clean water to drink every day?" put people in defensive mode; they will think they are being manipulated. Or worse, that yes is a telltale sign for you to leave them alone.

No-oriented questions make people comfortable; they give a sense of control to the one who says it — "I'm in charge here." That's what no-oriented questions do to your mind. "Do you like the performance of this government?", "Is this a bad time to talk?", "Should we just stop here and not go further?" It allows the other party to intervene and correct, which seems to put them in charge and makes them comfortable.

No is not the end of negotiation, it's the start. It helps all parties bring out more facts to the table so we can discuss them.

## Trigger the two words

Getting the other party to say "That's right" is like magic. It's a telltale sign that you have listened to them and captured the motivation behind their actions. You can achieve this in steps:

1. Let them talk, don't interrupt; if they pause, mirror.
2. Label a feeling and an action they took. Acknowledge those.
3. Paraphrase — repeat what they told you back to them in your own words.
4. Summarize — paraphrasing + labeling makes a summary. You must make them feel heard, capture everything you listened to and repeat it back to them.

"That's right" is good, but beware "you're right" — that signals they are not interested in what you said.
